Áskesis —from the Greek Ἄσκησις, “training”— is a philosophy of training the whole person: a way of living with less waste, more self-mastery and a life ordered around Christ. It doesn't train only the body. It trains habits, will and character.

I

Training the whole person

What Áskesis is, what it diagnoses, and why it doesn't ask you to do more, but to need less.

Áskesis starts from a paradox: we have never had so many tools to organize our lives, and yet we keep living with scatter, overload, anxiety and no margin. A person can keep an immaculate calendar, stick to their habits and hit their goals, and still be moving in the wrong direction. The problem isn't always disorder in execution. It can be disorder in what we choose.

Hence the distinction that organizes the whole project: efficiency is not direction. Efficiency asks how to get a result with fewer resources; direction asks whether that result is worth getting. When the course is wrong, efficiency doesn't fix the problem: it amplifies it. That is why Áskesis doesn't begin by asking “what else do I need?” but “how much of all this is actually necessary?”

And beneath it all, one observation: whatever is repeated, forms. There is no neutral zone in a life. Every habit, every purchase, every “yes,” every “no” and every small promise kept is training something in us. The question isn't whether we are being formed, but whether that training is deliberate or accidental.

Its three pillars are named by three verbs, kept in their original Spanish as the project's motto: Simplifica —simplify—, Cumple —fulfill your duty—, Consagra —consecrate.

Simplifica.
Frugality

What is truly necessary?

Use little, need little, waste little. Tell the essential from the superfluous. It doesn't seek the minimum: it seeks enough.

Its fruit:freedom
Cumple.
Discipline

What is mine to do?

Do what is yours to do even when you don't feel like it, governing yourself. Not harshness, not perfectionism: it asks for faithfulness, not omnipotence.

Its fruit:reliability
Consagra.
Faith

Toward whom is all of this ordered?

Discern, reconsecrate, offer and trust. Not an add-on to a life already organized: the center from which the whole life is reorganized.

Its fruit:peace
It isn't only about what we obtain, but about who we become while we do it.

The pillars correct one another

Every virtue warps when it is separated from its counterweight. None of them, alone, is enough.

  • DisciplinewithoutFrugality→Unnecessary activity, carried out with great discipline.
  • DisciplinewithoutFaith→Self-sufficiency: believing it all depends on willpower.
  • FrugalitywithoutFaith→Cutting for the sake of cutting, without knowing what it frees you for.
  • FaithwithoutDiscipline→Passivity: waiting for God to do what was ours to do.
  • FaithwithoutFrugality→Pious scatter: good works that crowd out what is ours to do.

That is why service is not a fourth pillar. It is the fruit of all three. Self-mastery isn't the destination; it is the instrument for becoming available for the good: to love, to serve, to listen and to keep faith.

The philosophy is expressed through three vehicles. The book develops it in full and turns it into a Rule of Life. The video podcast trains it out loud: solo, in conversation and in interviews. The talk brings it into a specific room. And the Substack keeps it company, issue by issue. Áskesis wasn't written to be read once: it was written to be practiced.

Order your life before you optimize it.

Áskesis puts forward a simple but radical idea: we don't need a more optimized life, but a more ordered one. It questions the culture of “more” —more goals, tools, activity, results and control— and holds that a well-ordered life begins by discerning what truly deserves our time, our attention, our effort and our resources.

More than a productivity method, it is a philosophy of formation. Every decision produces two results: an external one —what we obtain— and an internal one —the person we become while obtaining it. A behavior can be productive and, at the same time, deforming. That is why the book doesn't only ask what a decision produces, but what it produces in you.

And it doesn't end in concepts. Its final part turns the philosophy into a Rule of Life —the day, the week, the month, the year, work, resources, the mind— with matrices, questions for moments of pressure, a template, a guide for groups and a forty-day training program.

Order before you optimize

The difference isn't how hard each one works, but the order in which each one decides.

Performance
do→measure→optimize→repeat
Áskesis
discern→order→simplify→optimize→practice→examine

Optimizing assumes that what you optimize deserves to exist. That makes optimization a secondary activity: a criterion has to come first. Rules serve for what was foreseen; criteria let you decide when something nobody anticipated arrives.

The architecture of an ordered life

Four levels. Disorder appears when one usurps the place of another.

  • iTelosWhat do I live for?The final end that gives meaning and hierarchy to everything else. In Áskesis, a life ordered around Christ.
  • iiGoalsWhat do I want to achieve?Concrete results that give direction. Pursued seriously, without turning them into gods.
  • iiiProcessesWhat must I practice?The stable practices through which goals are pursued and, above all, the person is formed.
  • ivActionsWhat do I do today?The concrete decisions that turn everything above into conduct.

A goal becomes an identity. An urgency becomes a priority. A tool becomes a purpose. Against that, one rule: the telos orders the goals, the goals orient the processes, and the processes organize the actions.

MISO
Minimum Input. Same Output.Spend the least; keep the result.

Frugality's operating principle. It doesn't cut indiscriminately: it finds the essential minimum input that actually moves the result and removes the rest. MISO doesn't lower the standard. It removes the waste. And the cost is never only money:

MoneyTimeEnergyAttentionEffortComplexityToolsUpkeep

The first form of simplifying is eliminating. There's no point automating a task that should disappear, or perfecting a meeting that shouldn't exist. And every decision is weighed twice: what does it produce? and what does it produce in me?

“When I have X, I'll be able to do Y.” → “With what I have today, how can I do Y?”
Goals produce results. Processes form people. The process is the Dojo: the place where you practice, repeat, fail, correct and persevere, until the extraordinary becomes ordinary.

The Áskesis Wheel

Order isn't a final state. It's a continuous practice. The wheel doesn't end: it begins again.

  1. ConsecratePlace your life under God.
  2. DiscernDetermine what is yours to do.
  3. EliminateRemove what doesn't deserve to stay.
  4. SimplifyReduce complexity and waste.
  5. OrderGive each thing its place.
  6. FulfillDo what is yours to do.
  7. TrainRepeat until it forms character.
  8. OfferHand over the effort.
  9. TrustLet go of what you don't control.
  10. ExamineReview and correct.
  11. And begin again.

What it promises, and what it refuses to promise

It doesn't promise control, comfort, guaranteed results or perfection. It promises an ordered life: one that can hold illness, fatigue, loss and defeat, but in which things keep their hierarchy. Four fruits follow from that order.

Freedom

Because whoever needs less depends on less.

Stability

Because a life built on duty doesn't depend on motivation.

Serenity

Because whoever tells responsibility from control stops carrying the impossible.

Availability

Because whoever recovers margin can use it to love and to serve.

What Áskesis is not

  • A productivity channel, or a source of hacks for doing more.
  • Fitness, or a cult of the body.
  • Minimalism as an aesthetic.
  • Harshness for its own sake, or suffering for its own sake.
  • Deprivation as an identity or a reason to feel superior.
  • Self-help for becoming the best version of yourself, by yourself.

Áskesis asks a great deal of action, and very little of the ego. It doesn't make results an identity, discipline a salvation, or the body a god.

IV

The talks

The in-person expression: companies, universities, teams, Catholic communities, congresses and gatherings.

An Áskesis talk is not a motivational speech, not a productivity class in Christian language, and not a summary of the book. It is a training session: an idea that gets examined, a distinction that gets learned, and a practice the audience can begin the next day.

The audience isn't defined by age or job title, but by a suspicion: that the problem isn't a lack of tools, but a lack of order. People who already do a great deal and are starting to ask how much of it deserves doing.

We don't come to teach you to do more. We come to help you do what is yours to do, and to leave the result in God's hands.

There are two ways to bring it into a room. The keynote leads: defined direction, tighter curation, greater intensity. The conversation accompanies: closer and more dialogical, with participation in service of the question. Neither is a lesser version of the other; each is a different relationship with the room.

The distinctions

What an Áskesis talk should help separate. Almost all waste begins by confusing one of these pairs.

  • Efficiency is not direction
  • More is not better
  • Enough is not minimum
  • Frugality is not deprivation
  • Discipline is not harshness
  • Motivation is not duty
  • Preparation is not action
  • Responsibility is not control
  • Success is not faithfulness
  • Failure is not worthlessness

Three to begin with

Foundational
Order your life before you optimize it

The full thesis: why efficiency without direction only gets you to the wrong place faster.

Criterion
MISO: minimum input, same output

Frugality as a discipline of perception: eliminate before you add, and don't optimize what doesn't deserve to exist.

Application
Do your best. Let God do the rest.

Discipline and Faith together: maximum commitment to action, minimum attachment to the result. Responsibility is not control.
