I

About Mario Garza Livas and this body of work

Who writes, what was written, and why the twenty-six books form a single body of work.

Mario Garza Livas is a strategist devoted to designing systems capable of sustaining performance under pressure: in the person, in the company, in the family’s wealth and in institutions. Founder and director of The Metanoia Group, strategic advisor, family office CIO, football business consultant, sports agent and adjunct professor, his professional practice spans domains that are rarely thought about together. This written work is where they are.

The twenty-seven books gathered here —twenty-eight PDF volumes, since Learning Oneself was published in two parts— are not a collection of scattered interests. They are the same investigation applied to different levels of reality: how a person learns, decides and governs herself; how an expert transfers judgment to someone else; how a company is born, operates, decouples from its founder and allocates its capital; how artificial intelligence changes what an organization is able to attempt; how wealth becomes the capacity to choose; how a structure survives what it failed to anticipate; how power keeps its purpose when no one is watching; and from which center all of the above is ordered.

That unity is not rhetorical — it is verifiable. Concepts migrate from one book to the next and mean the same thing in all of them. The test of absence —what remains when whoever holds the system together is gone— is the final criterion of Second Mind, the decisive question of Business Architecture, the diagnostic instrument of The Gradual Window and the governance standard of The Ship Is Not the Voyage. Stewardship is the corrective principle of The Great Inversion, the final transformation of You Don’t Control the Harvest and the wealth framework of Thy Will Be Done. The sequence BEING → DOING → HAVING structures both ineXorable and Thy Will Be Done. Reading two books of this corpus means starting the third with an advantage.

The underlying thesis running through all of them can be stated in one sentence: sustainable advantage does not come from extraordinary effort or isolated talent, but from the architecture capable of turning potential into realized capacity, reproducibly and over time. Hence the corpus insists on shifting attention from the outcome to the system that produces it, from prediction to the structure that absorbs error, and from accumulation to the question that makes it intelligible: capacity for what?

Two of these books —Football Intelligence and Zero Margin— also belong to the Metanoia Football corpus, where they sit alongside twenty-five applied research papers on professional football. Two others —ineXorable and Thy Will Be Done— gave rise to projects of their own that outgrew the book format. The rest live here. All are downloadable in PDF, free of charge, with no registration and nothing asked in return.

A note on language. Every book was written and published in Spanish, and the PDFs on this page are the Spanish editions. Titles, theses and abstracts have been translated here so that readers can judge whether a book is worth their time; English editions do not yet exist. Today’s AI tools translate and summarize a full book competently, and readers who do not speak Spanish are encouraged to use them.

II

The shared vocabulary

Eight concepts that recur throughout the corpus and mean the same thing in every book. They are the evidence that the twenty-seven titles share a single intellectual architecture.

Architecture

The set of structures, mechanisms and relationships that determines what a system can produce on a sustained basis. As against the one-off intervention — the advice, the effort, the isolated decision — architecture is what keeps working when no one is pushing. The entire corpus shifts attention from the act to the architecture that makes it possible or prevents it.

Learning Oneself · Decision Architecture · Business Architecture · ineXorable · Zero Margin

Capacity

What a system can do, for how long, and with how much independence. It is the unit of measurement the corpus proposes in place of the outcome, the figure or the inventory. A company that grows without becoming more capable has not progressed; larger wealth that sustains less time is not greater wealth; learning that does not survive outside the classroom did not happen.

Money Is a Promise · The Impossible Company · Space of Action · Earned Complexity

The test of absence

The most demanding criterion in the corpus: what keeps working once the person who held the system together is gone. Applied to the expert, it measures whether he installed capacity or dependence. Applied to the founder, it reveals whether he built a company or a bottleneck. Applied to wealth or to an institution, it separates what was designed from what was merely sustained.

Second Mind · Business Architecture · The Gradual Window · The Ship Is Not the Voyage

Margin

The distance between what a system can withstand and what is being demanded of it. Liquidity, slack, redundancy, uncommitted time: margin is not inefficiency, it is the material infrastructure of freedom. Without margin every decision becomes forced and error stops being correctable.

ineXorable · The Gradual Window · Autonomy · Money Is a Promise

Calibrated judgment

The ability to decide well when information is incomplete, time is short and error is irreversible. It is not certified by knowledge or credentials: it is forged through prolonged exposure to environments where review, delay and the absorption of error simply do not exist — and it can be transferred to other domains, or faked.

Zero Margin · Decision Architecture · The Compound Interest of Judgment · Football Intelligence

Stewardship

The inversion of one's relationship to what one holds: whoever has power, capital or decision-making authority receives them not only as a prerogative but as a responsibility toward those who bear the consequences and those who will inherit what is built. It is the point where the corpus stops being technical and becomes explicitly moral.

The Great Inversion · You Don't Control the Harvest · Thy Will Be Done · The Ship Is Not the Voyage

System over willpower

No serious architecture can run on personal discipline as its operating system. When performance depends on someone not failing — the founder, the student, the advisor, the ruler — fragility is already built in. The corpus designs environments, mechanisms and governance precisely so as not to depend on heroes.

Learning Oneself · The Varnish of Character · Who Governs? · You Don't Control the Harvest

BEING → DOING → HAVING

The order in which the corpus insists on working: first who the person is and from which center she decides, then what she builds and how, and only then what she accumulates and what for. Inverting the sequence — organizing a life around having — is the shared diagnosis of several of these books.

ineXorable · Thy Will Be Done · The Center That Does Not Move · The Four Gardens

III

The seventeen categories

No book in this corpus belongs to a single subject, and forcing it into one would impoverish what it says. Each work therefore carries the labels that actually describe it: in green those for which it is a central text, in amber those to which it contributes without being its main concern.

Primary label — the book is a central text on that subject.
Secondary label — the book contributes to that subject without being its main concern.
A
Learning and Cognitive Capacity
1 primary · 4 secondary

This category deals with the competence that precedes every other one: becoming competent at what you do not yet know how to do. It is not a subject about study, memory or personal productivity. It is a subject about control: who decides what gets learned, what evidence verifies that it was learned, and what happens once instruction disappears.

The shift these texts propose is the same one that runs through the corpus, applied to the mind: stop treating learning as the accumulation of information and start treating it as the design of a self-correcting control system. Hence the insistence on defining performance before studying, on deconstructing skills rather than topics, on retrieving instead of rereading, on using error as a diagnostic instrument rather than a verdict, and on designing the environment, because willpower can never be the operating system.

Its success criterion is deliberately uncomfortable for whoever teaches: the architecture works when the learner no longer needs it. That is why the category bears directly on pedagogy and advisory — an expert who does not produce autonomy produces dependence — and also on character, since an identity that breaks with error cannot sustain any long cycle of learning.

SecondaryThe Varnish of Character · The Door Between Two Minds · Second Mind · Who Governs?
B
Character and Interior Life
2 primary · 6 secondary

Here the work is on the substrate from which a person responds before having deliberated: the automatic patterns that govern reaction, interpretation and self-treatment. The central thesis is that the object of personal development is not the outcome, nor some supposed better version of oneself, but the character from which one lives, decides and responds to what happens.

The conceptual apparatus comes from integrating classical Stoicism with attention training: constructs — learned interpretations that keep operating even after the conditions that produced them are gone —, the widening of the dichotomy of control into control, influence and non-control, and the distinction between feeling an emotion and obeying it. The metaphor of varnish sums up the method: there is no transformation by a single insight, only successive layers of attention, discernment, choice and practice until new responses become stable dispositions.

It is the category with the widest secondary presence in the corpus, and not by accident. Character shows up wherever a system depends on a person: the learner who must tolerate frustration without quitting, the interlocutor willing to be changed by a conversation, the person discerning a vocation without being governed by impatience, the one who works with excellence without demanding a harvest from life, and the one who administers power without letting the result replace the purpose.

C
Attention, Desire and Self-Government
1 primary · 3 secondary

There is a question that precedes every decision, every strategy and every architecture: who governs. Not who is in charge of an organization, but who actually directs a person the moment an impulse appears. This category deals with that frontier — desire, attention, habit, environment — and does so at a historical moment when the capture of attention has stopped being a side effect of technological design and become its explicit objective.

Its thesis breaks with the two usual answers. Against moralism, it holds that desiring is not obeying and that the problem is not solved by eliminating desire. Against voluntarism, it holds that willpower is a secondary tool and never the main architecture: when behavior depends on someone not failing at half past eleven at night, the system was already badly designed. What does work is a different chain — seeing the patterns, interrupting them early, ordering the environment, integrating desire into a fuller life and transcending mere abstention — sustained by real relationships and by character understood as something manufactured rather than declared.

The category closes where the rest of the corpus begins. Governing oneself is not the goal: it is the condition. A truly free person is not only the one who can say "no" to what enslaves her, but the one who, having recovered self-government, can say "yes" to what she has decided deserves her life. Without that capacity installed, none of the architectures the other books propose — company, wealth, work, vocation — has anything to stand on.

D
Vocation, Work and Trajectory
3 primary · 2 secondary

What a professional life builds, on what evidence its direction is decided, and what remains when the trajectory ends. The three central books of this category attack the same error from three different flanks: treating vocation as a discovery, reputation as accumulated visibility, and the outcome as a debt that reality is obliged to pay.

Against the first, the proposal is to replace the fortune teller with the farmer: vocation is not discovered once and for all, it is cultivated across four observable dimensions — purpose, enjoyment, competence and viability — while clearly distinguishing mission from livelihood, exploring before concentrating and accumulating evidence before committing. Against the second, the proposal is one's own voice as an asset: a position excavated from real experience rather than manufactured for attention, one that gains strength over time and does not depend entirely on any company, title or platform. Against the third, the fundamental asymmetry between what is sown and what is reaped.

The common thread is precise responsibility: owning the process completely — craft, word, habits, excellence — without confusing it with a control over the outcome that was never there. Which is why all three books end up shifting toward the question of whom one builds for, and on what terms what was built is handed over.

E
Conversation and Human Connection
1 primary · 1 secondary

The smallest category in the corpus and, arguably, the one with the most at stake. It deals with the real encounter between two people: not the exchange of information, but the conversation capable of producing something neither of them would have reached alone. The distinction between communicating and conversing is not semantic; it is the difference between transmitting and being transformed.

The diagnosis is contemporary and structural: the attention economy, polarization, the instrumentalization of the other and the disappearance of common spaces have made enormously expensive a capacity we used to take for granted. The answer is not conversational technique or good manners, but far more demanding conditions: sustained attention, tolerance of silence, intellectual vulnerability, disagreement without destruction, and the willingness to understand without understanding meaning conceding.

Its link to the rest of the corpus is direct. The transfer of judgment between an expert and whoever consults him happens inside a conversation or it does not happen at all; the diagnosis that precedes any serious intervention depends on the quality of the questions; and the attention treated here as a scarce resource is the same one that appears elsewhere as cognitive capital and strategic infrastructure.

F
Judgment and Decision Architecture
2 primary · 10 secondary

The technical core of the corpus, and the category with the widest reach: nearly every other book passes through here. Its object is not the individual decision but the system that produces decisions. A correct decision may come from a fragile architecture — and then it is not repeatable — just as a well-designed system turns even its errors into the capacity to decide better next time.

Hence a set of distinctions that recurs throughout the corpus: process rather than outcome as the criterion for judging a decision; context rather than memory; adaptive capacity rather than isolated accuracy; an uncertainty map as a mandatory part of every important recommendation. And hence too the thesis on calibration: high-level judgment is not certified by knowledge or credentials but forged through prolonged exposure to environments where review, delay and the absorption of error disappear — with the corresponding warning about false transfer and survivorship bias.

The practical consequence is an inversion of the usual question. Not "what should I decide?", but "how good is the system I am deciding from, and what is it learning from what I already decided?". This is the category that connects the company to the family's wealth, advisory to artificial intelligence, and elite football to ordinary life.

G
Advisory and the Transfer of Expertise
4 primary · 1 secondary

What it actually means to help someone decide better. The category brings together four complementary answers: a doctrine of intervention, an economic critique of the traditional advisory model, a technological architecture to sustain it, and a calibration standard for judging whoever advises.

The governing principle is counterintuitive: the expert's work does not consist in transmitting knowledge or delivering answers, but in transforming the architecture from which another person understands, decides and acts. That requires diagnosing before intervening, designing backward from the outcome, using friction as an instrument of understanding rather than a display of power, and verifying change with evidence instead of impressions. Its final metric is the test of absence: what remains once the expert is gone.

To that doctrine is added a structural diagnosis: if the accumulated knowledge about a principal is the most valuable asset in the relationship, the episodic model of sessions lets it depreciate between one meeting and the next. The proposal — context capital, living memory, continuous presence, multi-agent deliberation and cross-domain integration — turns that knowledge into an asset that compounds. And the selection criterion hardens: an advisor's calibration is not detected in a good presentation, but in what already survived the storm.

H
Football and Environments of Irreversible Error
1 primary · 1 secondary

Elite professional football appears in this corpus for a methodological reason, not a biographical one: it is one of the few environments where error admits no review, cannot be postponed and is not absorbed by the organization. That absence of subsidies makes it a privileged laboratory for studying how judgment under pressure is forged — and how it is faked.

Two contributions follow. One is technical: understanding analytics not as a collection of data and models but as decision engineering, where causality, explicit uncertainty, governance, adoption and ex-post evaluation weigh as much as model sophistication. The other is epistemological: the seven capabilities forged by a zero-margin environment, from structural diagnosis and irreversible decision-making to reading power and value engineering under hard constraints.

These two books also belong to the Metanoia Football corpus, where they sit alongside twenty-five applied research papers on the professional football ecosystem. Here they work as a bridge: they show where much of the vocabulary of the rest of the work comes from, and why a sporting environment has something serious to teach a company, a family's wealth and whoever advises them.

I
Entrepreneurship and Early Construction
2 primary

This category exists because a company that does not yet exist and a company already operating pose different problems, and treating them with the same manual is one of the most expensive mistakes available. The subject here is the move from zero to one: what to build, in what order, and on what evidence one earns the right to commit resources.

The central concept is earned complexity. An organization should not become more complex because it grew, because it raised capital or because other companies operate that way: structure, processes, people and systems must be added in response to a need reality has already demonstrated. Hence the discipline of distinguishing inherited, copied and earned complexity, of managing uncertainty through minimal reversible experiments, of treating optionality as the real asset while doubt persists, and of removing whatever has stopped justifying itself.

The second front is the transformation of the entrepreneurial craft itself once intelligence stops being scarce. If a capability no longer requires people, a department and a structure, but access and a minimal architecture, the question shifts from what can be attempted to what deserves to be attempted. There the new scarcity is not intelligence: it is judgment, attention, memory and commitment.

PrimaryEarned Complexity · Space of Action
J
Directing Operating Companies
2 primary · 6 secondary

The counterpart to the previous category: the company that already operates, already bills and already has a history. Its problem is not what to build, but why the system produces what it produces — and why it still depends on the extraordinary effort of whoever runs it. The shift these books propose is from operator to architect: stop pushing people and solving problems one by one, and start designing the system that produces the results.

That requires seeing the company as a complete economic system, where profit can be an illusion if it never turns into cash, the constraint determines throughput while everything else determines cost, the average hides failure at the extremes, automation amplifies the existing design instead of correcting it, culture is the behavior that gets tolerated rather than the speech that gets delivered, and all retained capital is invested even when no one decided to invest it.

The second line of work changes the very unit of organizational design: stop thinking first in terms of jobs and start thinking in terms of capabilities, distributing execution, coordination and judgment across people and digital systems under criteria of measurement, accountability and traceability. In both cases the final test is the same, and it is the test of absence applied to leadership: what happens to this company if its director stops pushing.

K
Family Business, Ownership and Succession
1 primary

The family business deserves its own category because it contains systems that obey incompatible logics and that usually operate as if they were one. The family runs on unconditional belonging; the business, on demonstrated competence; ownership, on property rights; governance, on fiduciary responsibility. Confusing them is what turns a source of prosperity into a source of conflict.

The answer is structural before it is emotional: boards that govern rather than ratify, a family protocol that works as a constitution and not as a declaration of good intentions, exit mechanisms that exist before they are needed, succession understood as preparation and letting go, and a system designed for the crisis rather than for the quiet days. Goodwill is not a system of governance, and the surname grants belonging but does not guarantee competence.

Its final thesis is the one that gives it its name and connects it to the rest of the corpus: the company is the ship, not the voyage. The decisive indicator is not the survival of the business, but the freedom, relationships and purpose of the people who sustain it — which opens, in its second half, the move from good governance to stewardship.

PrimaryThe Ship Is Not the Voyage
L
Artificial Intelligence and Augmented Capacity
3 primary · 3 secondary

Artificial intelligence appears in this corpus as a structural variable, not as a productivity tool. The question is not which tasks can be automated, but what changes in an organization when intellectual capacity stops being scarce: what it can attempt, with what structure, under what governance and with what new risks.

The shared thesis is that strategic value does not reside in the model but in the architecture built around it: long-term memory, data as cognitive infrastructure, agents whose autonomy is proportional to risk, traceability and auditability as conditions of trust, a deliberately designed point where the human intervenes, and an economics that governs automation instead of chasing it. Autonomy is bought with governance; automation has to be earned with evidence.

Hence the warnings that run through the category: fluency is not truth, synthetic progress can simulate advance with no contact with reality, an abundance of options produces paralysis without a criterion for discarding, and the worst possible architecture is a fair of agents with no integration. Real leverage is not saving time but increasing capacity — and the competitive frontier will not be having artificial intelligence, but governing it better.

M
Wealth and Economic Autonomy
2 primary · 5 secondary

Wealth treated as a system of governance rather than a collection of investments. The category replaces the usual question — how much do I have — with one that changes the whole analysis: what can I do, for how long, and with how much independence. Money stops being the territory and becomes a promise of future capacity, subject to a reality it does not control.

On that basis decisions fall into order: survival precedes optimization, preparation beats prediction, liquidity is a reserve of freedom before it is an opportunity cost, slack is an investment rather than an inefficiency, genuine diversification means diversifying promises rather than counting assets, debt is a decision about the future that commits options, and a decision must be judged by its process before its outcome is known.

The final destination of this architecture is explicit and it is not financial. Wealth exists to produce options: time, room to maneuver and the capacity to choose without depending blindly on markets, institutions or advisors. Which is why the category always arrives at the same question — capacity for what? — and at the notion of sufficiency as a deliberate limit on accumulation, as well as at the problem of continuity: wealth that only works while its owner is alive is incomplete.

N
Uncertainty, Fragility and Antifragility
2 primary · 6 secondary

This category deliberately abandons the terrain of prediction. Its premise is that major ruptures rarely create fragility: they reveal it. Before the collapse there is a period — the gradual window — in which dependencies deepen, margins narrow and fragilities accumulate quietly while everything appears to be working normally.

The object of management is therefore not the event but the structure: the capacity to absorb, to wait, to choose, to adapt and to continue. That is built with liquidity, redundancy, reversibility, real diversification measured at the level of the system rather than the inventory, and important decisions partly made before the pressure arrives. Contemporary risk lives in the interactions more than in the pieces, and size does not equal resilience.

Its most ambitious version turns uncertainty into a design problem across all three levels — person, work and wealth: identifying dependencies, preserving margin, developing transferable capabilities and building systems that not only withstand change but learn from it. The conclusion is neither prudence nor invulnerability: it is capacity. Surviving by design rather than by inertia, keeping the ability to decide precisely when what could not be foreseen arrives.

O
Power, Institutions and Stewardship
2 primary · 4 secondary

Here the corpus moves up in scale and examines collective systems: companies, markets, states, democratic institutions. Two structural pathologies organize the category. The first is the inversion: the moment when results stop being a consequence of purpose and start replacing it. It happens without villains — it is enough for incentives, metrics and decision structures to push reasonable people, step by step, toward behavior that contradicts the original end.

The second is outdatedness: institutions designed to solve the problems of a world whose conditions have already changed. The answer is not to modernize or digitize them, but to distinguish rigorously between the function they must preserve and the historical form through which they tried to fulfill it, and to accept that every new capability must come with a new safeguard.

The corrective principle the category proposes is stewardship: whoever decides receives power not merely as a prerogative but as a responsibility proportional to the radius of consequences of those decisions, toward those who bear them and those who will inherit what is built. Translated into institutional design, that means truth as infrastructure, reversibility and experimentation, memory capable of producing learning without becoming an instrument of persecution, and systems that keep doing the right thing when no one is watching and their creators are long gone.

P
Faith, Transcendence and Meaning
3 primary · 3 secondary

The category where the corpus declares its foundation. Its starting point is a diagnosis that offers no consolation: a person can be successful, productive, disciplined and even religiously observant, and still remain interiorly disordered if she has placed her center in performance, control, money, approval or in herself. The proposal is not to improve a life, but to displace its center of gravity.

Three distinct works follow. One is the architecture of an ordered interior life: an identity received rather than conquered, discernment without absolute control, suffering, sacramental anchoring, ordinary life as the place of holiness, and a rule of life that turns principles into sustainable rhythms. Another is the examination of a specific phenomenon — Garabandal — reconstructed with historical, theological and spiritual rigor, carefully separating documented facts, the author's hypotheses and defined doctrine, and shifting the focus from the expectation of future events to conversion in the present.

The third is integration: ordering a single life — person, vocation, work, money, authority and relationships — under one question, discerning God's will before optimizing results. It is also the category where the corpus most clearly accepts the price of its convictions: the real test of a conviction is its cost, and freedom is measured too by what one is capable of renouncing.

Q
Integration: BEING, DOING and HAVING
2 primary · 2 secondary

The last category does not group a subject: it groups an ambition. It gathers the works that refuse to treat the person, her work and her wealth as separate compartments, and that propose governing them under a single architecture. These are the books that would least tolerate a single label, because their object is precisely the seam between all the others.

The shared diagnosis is fragmentation: living by different criteria depending on the domain — one ethics for the family, another for the business, another for money — until decisions stop answering to a single orientation. The correction proposed is an order, not a list: first being — who the person is and from which center she decides —, then doing — what she builds and by what criteria —, and only then having — what she accumulates, for what purpose and under what responsibility.

Each work walks that sequence from a different foundation. One does it through design: structure, purpose and adaptation as the three dimensions of any architecture capable of absorbing change and turning it into the freedom to act. The other does it through faith: discern before optimizing, and administer faithfully what has been received. They agree on the result they seek — a coherent life — and on what they reject: the promise of control over the outcome.

Learning Oneself

Original Spanish title: Aprenderse

True mastery is not knowing everything, but knowing how to begin when something appears that we do not yet know how to do.

Learning and Cognitive CapacityAdvisory and Transfer of ExpertiseCharacter and Interior LifeAttention, Desire and Self-Government

Learning Oneself proposes a simple but far-reaching idea: the most valuable competence is not accumulating knowledge, but learning to become competent at what one does not yet know. Across two volumes, the book develops a system for turning curiosity into capacity: defining what you want to achieve, deconstructing a skill, practicing deliberately, retrieving instead of rereading, using error as information, diagnosing your own gaps and testing your learning in real situations.

But the proposal goes beyond learning a particular subject better. Its ultimate aim is to build a self-updating system: a way of learning that improves with every new thing learned. The reader moves from being a consumer of information to being the architect of his own learning, able to use teachers, institutions and tools —artificial intelligence included— without becoming dependent on them. In the end, Learning Oneself argues that real mastery is not knowing everything, but knowing how to begin when something appears that we still cannot do.

Decision Architecture

Original Spanish title: Arquitectura de Decisión

A correct decision can come from a fragile system; a well-designed system turns even its errors into the capacity to decide better.

Judgment and Decision ArchitectureArtificial Intelligence and Augmented CapacityDirecting Operating Companies

Decision Architecture starts from a simple but profound premise: in environments that are increasingly complex, uncertain and fast-moving, the real problem is not only making good decisions, but designing the system from which those decisions are produced. The book proposes moving from a one-off view of the decision to an integral architecture that incorporates information, diversity of perspectives, adversarial deliberation, memory, learning, adaptation and governance. Its aim is to show why an apparently correct decision may come from a fragile system and why, conversely, a well-designed system can turn every decision —its errors included— into the capacity to decide better in the future.

From that framework, the text develops practical tools for assessing and strengthening decision architecture at the personal, organizational and technological levels, including the use of artificial intelligence as a second mind: not as a substitute for human judgment, but as infrastructure for widening perception, preserving memory, challenging assumptions and accelerating learning. Its final thesis is that sustainable advantage does not necessarily belong to whoever has more information, more talent or better technology, but to whoever manages to build a system that systematically learns from its own decisions and becomes more capable over time.

Business Architecture

Original Spanish title: Arquitectura Empresarial

Stop asking how we do more, and start asking what we must design so the company is able to do more.

Directing Operating CompaniesWealth and Economic AutonomyJudgment and Decision Architecture

Business Architecture proposes a different way of understanding how to run a company: not as the art of pushing people, solving problems one at a time or chasing results, but as the discipline of designing the system that produces those results. The book walks through the company as an economic and operating system —from value creation and strategy to cash, operations, people, growth and capital allocation— to show why many organizations grow without becoming more capable, generate profit without generating cash, or delegate responsibilities without achieving genuine autonomy.

Its central proposal is the move from operator to architect: identifying the constraints that actually govern the system, intervening in causes rather than symptoms, turning strategy into concrete mechanisms, and building an organization able to produce results without permanently depending on the extraordinary effort of its director. Ultimately, the book invites replacing the question «how do we do more?» with a far more powerful one: «what must we design so that the company can do more, better and sustainably?»

Augmented Advisory

Original Spanish title: Asesoría Aumentada

The strategic value of artificial intelligence lies not in the intelligence of the model, but in the architecture built around it.

Artificial Intelligence and Augmented CapacityAdvisory and Transfer of ExpertiseDirecting Operating CompaniesPower, Institutions and Stewardship

Augmented Advisory presents an integral view of artificial intelligence that goes beyond the use of generative models as simple productivity tools. The work explores how to build systems capable of combining AI, long-term memory, data, autonomous agents and specialized knowledge in order to turn professional advisory into a continuous, contextual and scalable capability.
